## Introduction
The automotive industry is entering a period where data interoperability is becoming as important as vehicle interoperability.

Regulations such as EU Data Act introduce new expectations regarding access to data generated by using a connected vehicle, while customers, ecosystem partners, and service providers increasingly expect consistent and scalable methods for integrating with vehicle ecosystems.

For Automotive OEMs, this raises a practical question:
**How can vehicle-generated data be exposed, shared, and consumed in a way that is scalable, interoperable, and future-proof?**

The EU Data Act does not mandate specific technical standards (yet), but OEMs that choose common industry specifications can reduce implementation complexity, improve ecosystem interoperability, and accelerate delivery of compliant data-sharing capabilities. 

This document describes why we encourage the use of selected COVESA specifications and how OEMs can begin incorporating them into their EU Data Act strategies. It builds upon our previous recommendation to consider COVESA Vehicle Signal Specification and Vehicle Information Service Specification for vehicle data exchange. 

## Why Vehicle Data Standardization Matters Now
The automotive industry has historically evolved through proprietary interfaces, proprietary vehicle data models, and OEM-specific integration approaches. While this has enabled differentiation between manufacturers, it has also created significant barriers for scalable interoperability within  ecosystems.

The EU Data Act creates additional momentum for addressing these challenges. Vehicle users will increasingly expect to share vehicle-generated data with third parties of their choosing, and third-party service providers will expect efficient access mechanisms that work consistently across manufacturers.

At the same time, regulatory developments related to cybersecurity, digital resilience, privacy, and data governance continue to increase pressure on OEMs to establish well-governed and transparent data-sharing architectures.

This changing landscape creates a strong business case for adopting open and widely accepted specifications rather than creating new proprietary approaches for every new integration scenario.

## Industry Momentum
The increasing needs to meet new business demands and regulatory requirements have created a momentum for specific work on open source industry standards and specifications such as COVESA VSS and VISS. One example is the the AUTOSAR Automotive API Gateway specification that now builds upon COVESA VSS and VISS, demonstrating alignment with mainstream automotive software architectures:
> The vehicle data model defines syntax and semantics for the data that is accessible through the Automotive API. This model needs to be a common one, to allow unified interaction with the Automotive API across vehicles and manufacturers regardless of the internal representation of the data. The Vehicle Signal Specification (VSS) was chosen as the common vehicle data model.

> To facilitate access to the Automotive API a network interface, a communication protocol is required. The interface is to use the VSS vehicle data model. For that purpose, the Vehicle Information Service Specification version 2 (VISS) was chosen.

In parallel, COVESA and ACEA have established a collaboration to advance interoperability through standardized vehicle data models and APIs for commercial vehicles. 

Major automotive manufacturers, suppliers, and ecosystem participants - including BMW, Ford, Bosch, Geotab, and others - actively contribute to the development and evolution of COVESA specifications. Together, these initiatives demonstrate that VSS and VISS are no longer isolated community projects but increasingly form part of a broader industry effort to standardize vehicle data interoperability.
## Current Challenges in Vehicle Data Exchange
Most OEMs already possess sophisticated internal data architectures. However, when vehicle data must be exchanged outside organizational boundaries several challenges frequently emerge.
### Fragmented Data Definitions
The same business concept may be represented differently across OEMs, platforms, and suppliers. This creates translation effort and semantic ambiguity.
### Duplicate Integration Work
Third-party service providers often need to build and maintain separate integrations for each OEM despite requesting similar information from all of them.
### Slow Partner Onboarding
Proprietary APIs and data structures increase onboarding effort for ecosystem participants and slow down innovation cycles.
### Limited Data Portability
Without common data models, moving services, applications, and data consumers between platforms becomes costly and complex.
### Growing Compliance Cost
Regulatory requirements increasingly emphasize transparency, accessibility, and portability. Proprietary implementations often require significant additional effort to demonstrate compliance.

These challenges are not unique to the EU Data Act but become more visible and expensive as data-sharing expand.

## Solving the Data Model Challenge with VSS
The first challenge OEMs face is agreeing on the meaning of vehicle data.

The COVESA Vehicle Signal Specification (VSS) provides a common vocabulary and structure for describing vehicle-generated information. Rather than defining how data is transported, VSS focuses on what the data means.

Examples include:
- Vehicle speed.
- State of charge.
- Fuel level.
- Door status.
- Odometer readings.
- Environmental sensor values.

Using a shared data taxonomy provides several advantages:
- Reduced semantic ambiguity.
- Easier integration across organizations.
- Lower development and maintenance costs.
- Improved reusability of tools and applications.
- Stronger long-term interoperability.

Importantly, adopting VSS does not require OEMs to abandon existing internal data models. Many organizations begin by mapping existing signals and attributes to corresponding VSS definitions and extending the model where necessary. This enables a gradual transition while preserving existing engineering investments.

## Solving the Access and API Challenge with VISS
Once data concepts are aligned, the next challenge is providing access to that data.

The COVESA Vehicle Information Service Specification (VISS) addresses this challenge by defining a standard interface for accessing vehicle data.

VISS provides capabilities such as:
- Reading vehicle information.
- Writing supported values.
- Subscribing to vehicle events and updates.
- Fine-grained access control.
- Standardized communication patterns across implementations.

For OEMs working with EU Data Act requirements, VISS offers a practical foundation for implementing user-controlled access to vehicle data without requiring each manufacturer to design a completely unique API framework.

A common access mechanism also allows application developers and service providers to build reusable integrations that can operate across multiple vehicle brands.

## Getting Started Through Reference Architectures and Reference Implementations
For many organizations, the biggest challenge is not selecting a specification—it is understanding where to begin.

We encourage OEMs to start small and focus on practical implementation experience.
### **Step 1: Identify Relevant Vehicle Data**
Begin by identifying vehicle-generated data categories that are expected to be shared under current or future business scenarios.

### **Step 2: Map Existing Signals to VSS**
Evaluate how existing proprietary signal definitions correspond to VSS and identify any gaps requiring extensions.

### **Step 3: Establish a VISS Endpoint**
Implement a VISS-compatible interface that can expose selected data sets through controlled access mechanisms.

### **Step 4: Evaluate Reference Implementations**
Leverage existing open-source reference implementations and proof-of-concept environments to reduce development effort and accelerate learning.

A particularly useful starting point is the **COVESA Vehicle Information Server Reference implementation (VISSR)**.

VISSR provides an open-source implementation of the VISS specification and demonstrates how vehicle data, structured according to VSS, can be exposed through a standards-based API.

Using VISSR allows OEMs and ecosystem participants to:
- Gain practical experience with VISS without developing an implementation from scratch.
- Validate architectural decisions before introducing production-grade components.
- Experiment with authorization, access control, consent, and subscription-based data access models.
- Demonstrate interoperability between data producers and consumers using common specifications.
- Accelerate proof-of-concept and pilot activities related to EU Data Act data-sharing scenarios.

VISSR should not necessarily be viewed as a production platform, but rather as a reference implementation that illustrates how VSS and VISS can be combined into a working solution.

By leveraging VISSR, OEMs can validate VSS mappings, evaluate API governance models, explore consent and authorization patterns, and demonstrate interoperability with ecosystem partners before committing to production-scale implementations.

One example of how to implement VSS and VISS using VISSR is the demonstration of consent management at the COVESA All Members Meeting (AMM) in Gothenburg, Sweden, 2024. Links can be found below. 
##### **A Practical Reference Architecture Pattern**
A common adoption pattern is to introduce VISSR as a façade layer in front of existing vehicle data platforms and telematics infrastructures.

In this model:
1. Existing vehicle or backend systems remain unchanged.
2. Proprietary signals are mapped to VSS.
3. VISSR exposes selected data through a standardized VISS interface.
4. Third-party applications consume data through the common API rather than OEM-specific interfaces.

This incremental approach enables organizations to assess interoperability benefits while minimizing implementation risk and preserving existing platform investments.

### **Step 5: Expand Incrementally**
Start with a limited number of signals and use cases before scaling towards broader vehicle and ecosystem coverage.

The objective should not be a full architecture transformation from day one but rather the gradual adoption of common building blocks that support future interoperability.

## Adoption Journey: From Exploration to Ecosystem Interoperability
Adopting common specifications for vehicle data exchange does not need to be a large-scale transformation initiative from the outset. Many OEMs may choose to take an incremental approach, beginning with assessment and experimentation before moving towards broader deployment.

The following maturity model illustrates one possible adoption path for organizations seeking to leverage COVESA specifications within the context of the EU Data Act and future digital ecosystem initiatives.

| Phase      | Objective                                       | Key Activities                                                                        | Relevant COVESA Building <br>Blocks |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Assess     | Understand business and regulatory implications | Identify vehicle-generated data, evaluate obligations, analyze current data models    | VSS                                 |
| Experiment | Validate concepts and architecture choices      | Map proprietary signals, test interoperability, evaluate consent models               | VSS + VISSR                         |
| Pilot      | Implement limited production use cases          | Deploy APIs, onboard selected ecosystem partners, establish governance                | VSS + VISS                          |
| Scale      | Standardize external data access                | Expand data coverage, reuse integrations, establish common  onboarding patterns       | VSS + VISS                          |
| Ecosystem  | Enable cross-domain interoperability            | Connect vehicle data to mobility, energy, insurance, fleet, and smart city ecosystems | VSS + VISS + VDM                    |
##### **Assess**
The first step is understanding which vehicle-generated data may need to be shared and how existing data structures align with emerging industry practices.
##### **Experiment**
Once candidate datasets have been identified, OEMs can begin validating interoperability concepts through proof-of-concept implementations.

VISSR provides a practical environment for evaluating how VSS-based data can be made available through a VISS-compatible interface, allowing development teams to gain hands-on experience before making long-term platform decisions.
##### **Pilot**
Following successful experimentation, organizations may choose to implement pilot solutions focused on specific business cases, vehicle programs, or ecosystem partnerships.
##### **Scale**
As adoption grows, common specifications can help reduce duplication of effort across programs, brands, suppliers, and ecosystem partners. Standardized data models and interfaces improve interoperability while lowering integration costs and onboarding effort. 
##### **Ecosystem**
The long-term opportunity extends beyond regulatory compliance. As mobility ecosystems continue to evolve, richer semantic models and cross-domain interoperability become increasingly important.

Emerging initiatives such as the COVESA Vehicle Data Model (VDM) aim to support these broader ecosystem scenarios by extending vehicle data into a more comprehensive semantic framework. 
##### **A Pragmatic Approach**
It is important to note that organizations do not need to progress through all phases immediately. The objective of this model is not to prescribe a specific roadmap but to illustrate how OEMs can gradually adopt common specifications while preserving existing investments and minimizing implementation risk.

By starting with limited experiments and building upon proven standards, OEMs can establish a foundation for both near-term EU Data Act compliance and longer-term ecosystem interoperability.

## Extending Vehicle Data into Broader Ecosystems
Vehicle-generated data increasingly participates in larger digital ecosystems involving:
- Fleet operations.
- Insurance services.
- Charging infrastructure.
- Energy management.
- Smart cities.
- Mobility platforms.
- Maintenance and aftermarket services.

As these ecosystems become more interconnected, the value of common semantics grows.

This is one reason why developments such as the COVESA Vehicle Data Model (VDM) are attracting increasing attention. VDM extends beyond signal taxonomies and aims to provide richer semantic relationships between data concepts across domains.

Organizations adopting VSS and VISS today are likely to be better positioned to participate in these future ecosystem initiatives

## Business Benefits of Adopting Common Specifications
While regulatory compliance is often the initial driver, the long-term value extends significantly beyond regulation.

OEMs that adopt common data-sharing specifications can potentially realize benefits such as:
##### **Faster Delivery**
Reuse of existing tools, integrations, and expertise shortens implementation cycles.
##### **Lower Integration Costs**
Common specifications reduce the need for repeated customization and translation efforts.
##### **Improved Interoperability**
Partners can interact with multiple OEMs using a consistent approach.
##### **Stronger Ecosystem Participation**
Open and shared data models enable broader collaboration and innovation.
##### **Reduced Vendor Lock-In**
Portable data structures and interfaces create greater architectural flexibility.
##### **Future Readiness**
Adoption of industry-supported specifications positions organizations to respond more effectively to evolving regulatory and market expectations.

## Technical Enablers Rather Than Compliance Solutions!
It is important to recognize that VSS and VISS are not compliance frameworks and should not be viewed as a substitute for legal, security, privacy, or governance activities.

Implementing these specifications does not in itself make an organization compliant with the EU Data Act. Rather, they provide technical building blocks that can help OEMs establish standardized and interoperable approaches for representing and exposing vehicle-generated data. 

Additional capabilities remain necessary, including:
- User identity verification.
- Consent and authorization management.
- Privacy controls.
- Cybersecurity governance.
- Audit and monitoring capabilities.
- Commercial and contractual arrangements.
- Data quality and lifecycle management.

These capabilities must be implemented as part of the overall data-sharing architecture and governance model.

## Conclusion
The EU Data Act presents a significant opportunity for the automotive industry to improve data interoperability while enabling new services and business models.

Although no single technical approach is mandated, OEMs can significantly reduce complexity and accelerate ecosystem adoption by leveraging established industry specifications.

COVESA VSS provides a practical and widely supported approach for standardizing vehicle data definitions. COVESA VISS offers a corresponding mechanism for consistent data access, while VISSR provides a practical way to evaluate and validate architectural approaches before large-scale deployment. Together, these specifications provide a foundation upon which OEMs can build scalable, interoperable, and future-oriented vehicle data sharing capabilities. 

As vehicle data becomes increasingly connected to broader mobility ecosystems, initiatives such as COVESA VDM may further extend the value of standardized semantics beyond individual vehicles and organizations, helping create a more interoperable and innovative automotive data ecosystem.
